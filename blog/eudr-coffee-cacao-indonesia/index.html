EUDR and Indonesian Coffee and Cacao:
What EU Importers Need From Their Supplier

H
Hindia Timur Editorial
Sourcing and Compliance
November 26, 2026

From 30 December 2026, an EU company that imports Indonesian green coffee or cocoa beans must be able to prove that the crop was grown on land not deforested after 31 December 2020, that it was produced legally, and exactly where it was grown. That proof comes from the supplier. Under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), the importer files the due diligence statement, but the geolocation of every production plot, the harvest dates and the legality evidence all have to start at origin in Indonesia.

In short, an EU importer needs four things from an Indonesian coffee or cacao supplier: plot-level geolocation for every lot, a production date or date range, documents showing the crop was produced in line with Indonesian law, and a supply chain that keeps each lot separate from unverified product all the way to the port. This guide explains what changed in the December 2025 amendment, what applies to which products and when, and the questions to put to your supplier before your first 2027 shipment.

Where the EUDR stands today

The EUDR is Regulation (EU) 2023/1115. It covers seven commodities, including coffee and cocoa, and the products made from them that are listed in its Annex I. Its application has been postponed twice. The latest change, Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 of 19 December 2025, was published in the Official Journal on 23 December 2025 and set the current dates:

  • 30 December 2026: large and medium-sized operators and traders, and micro and small enterprises already covered by the EU Timber Regulation.
  • 30 June 2027: all other micro and small operators.

In May 2026 the European Commission published the simplification review that the amendment required. It said the Commission would not reopen the text of the regulation, and instead adjusted product scope through a delegated act and issued updated guidance. For coffee and cacao buyers, the core obligations are therefore settled: the December 2026 date stands, and the requirements below apply.

Which coffee and cacao products are covered

Annex I lists products by customs code. For coffee and cocoa, the codes most importers will deal with are these:

1801Cocoa beans, whole or broken, raw or roasted30 December 2026 (30 June 2027 for small operators)

Soluble coffee is the one change to coffee and cocoa scope. The Commission adopted a delegated act on 13 July 2026 adding it to Annex I, with application deferred to 30 December 2027. Published commentary reports the act as Delegated Regulation (EU) 2026/2102, in the Official Journal in September 2026. Ready-to-drink coffee beverages remain outside scope.

Who files what: importer, manufacturer and supplier

The December 2025 amendment narrowed who has to file. Only the operator that first places the product on the EU market -- in most cases the importer of green coffee or cocoa beans -- carries out full due diligence and submits a due diligence statement in the EU Information System. Companies further down the chain no longer repeat it.

RoleTypical exampleMain obligation
Operator (first placing)EU importer of green coffee or cocoa beansFull due diligence: collect information, assess and mitigate risk, submit a due diligence statement before customs release
Downstream operatorRoaster or chocolate maker using beans already covered by a statementNo new statement; keep supplier, customer and statement reference details for five years; larger companies register in the Information System
TraderDistributor reselling within the EUKeep the same records for five years; larger companies register
Indonesian supplierExporter, cooperative or estateNo direct legal obligation under the EUDR, but must supply the data the operator needs

The Commission's own guidance is clear that the regulation places no legal obligations on non-EU countries or producers. In practice that changes little. If the supplier cannot provide the data, the importer cannot file, and the shipment cannot be released for free circulation.

What the importer needs from the supplier

The information requirements in Article 9 translate into a short list of things a buyer should expect for every lot.

  • Geolocation of every plot: latitude and longitude with at least six decimal digits. For plots of more than 4 hectares, a polygon of the plot boundary is required. For smaller plots, a single point is accepted. Coordinates are usually uploaded as a GeoJSON file.
  • Date or time range of production: the harvest period for the lot.
  • Country and, where relevant, region of production: for Indonesia, typically province and district.
  • Deforestation-free evidence: information showing that none of the plots was deforested after 31 December 2020.
  • Legality evidence: documents showing the crop was produced in line with the relevant laws of Indonesia, covering land-use rights, environmental, labour and tax rules among others.
  • Quantity and description: net mass and, for coffee, whether green or roasted, matched to the commercial invoice.

The hardest of these is plot geolocation, because Indonesian coffee and cacao are overwhelmingly smallholder crops. Statistics Indonesia (BPS) figures cited by The Jakarta Post in November 2026 put the smallholder share at 99.6% of cocoa area and 98.6% of coffee area, much of it not yet registered. A single container of Sulawesi cocoa or Gayo Arabica can draw on hundreds of small plots, and each one has to be mapped.

A supplier who can only trace a lot to a collection centre or a cooperative warehouse cannot support an EUDR filing. Traceability has to reach the plot.

Indonesia's risk classification

The Commission's country benchmarking, set out in Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093 of 22 May 2025, classifies Indonesia as standard risk. Only Belarus, Myanmar, North Korea and Russia were classed as high risk. The classification matters in two ways:

  • No simplified due diligence: simplified due diligence is available only for products from low-risk countries. Importers of Indonesian coffee and cocoa must carry out the full risk assessment and mitigation steps.
  • Check rates: national authorities must check at least 3% of operators sourcing from standard-risk countries each year, compared with 1% for low-risk and 9% for high-risk countries.

Penalties are set by each Member State. The regulation requires maximum fines of at least 4% of the operator's annual EU turnover. The first review of the country classifications is foreseen for 2026, so importers should check the current list before each season.

Indonesia's preparation at origin

Indonesian authorities and industry have been building the data that EU buyers need. The main tool for smallholders is the cultivation registration certificate (STDB, Surat Tanda Daftar Budidaya), which records a farmer's plot and crop. Most smallholder coffee and cocoa land is not yet registered, and Indonesian analysts have called for mapping in these crops to be prioritised, so buyers should not assume that a registered lot is the norm.

For buyers, the practical point is that national registration helps, but it does not replace the supplier's own lot-level data. The due diligence statement needs coordinates for the plots that supplied this shipment, not a district-level total.

Questions to ask your supplier now

Before contracting for shipments that arrive after 30 December 2026, we suggest asking each Indonesian supplier the following, and asking for a sample data file rather than a written assurance.

  • Can you share plot-level coordinates for a recent lot, in GeoJSON, with polygons for plots over 4 hectares?
  • How do you keep mapped and unmapped product apart at collection, drying, hulling or fermentation, and in the warehouse?
  • What legality documents do you hold for land rights and farm registration, and can they be matched to the plots in the file?
  • How is deforestation-free status checked against the 31 December 2020 cut-off, and with which satellite or forest-cover data?
  • Who updates the data when farmers join or leave a cooperative, and how quickly?

For our own coffee, farm-level traceability with GPS-linked farm records is available for Arabica lots, while Robusta is typically traceable to cooperative level. That difference is exactly the kind of detail a buyer should confirm lot by lot. Our Indonesian coffee and Indonesian cacao pages set out origins and documentation, and when you request a quote you can state that the shipment must support an EUDR due diligence statement so that we confirm what geolocation data is available for the lots in question.

Frequently asked questions

When does the EUDR apply to Indonesian coffee and cocoa?

From 30 December 2026 for large and medium-sized operators and traders, and from 30 June 2027 for most micro and small operators. Soluble coffee is added to scope from 30 December 2027.

Does the Indonesian exporter submit the due diligence statement?

No. The statement is submitted by the operator that first places the product on the EU market, usually the EU importer. The exporter supplies the geolocation, production dates and legality information the importer needs to file it.

Do roasters and chocolate makers need their own due diligence statement?

Not when they use beans already covered by a statement. Since the December 2025 amendment, downstream operators and traders keep records of their suppliers, customers and statement reference numbers for five years, and larger companies must register in the EU Information System.

Is a single GPS point enough for a coffee or cacao farm?

For plots of 4 hectares or less, a single point with six decimal digits is accepted. For plots of more than 4 hectares, the regulation requires a polygon of the plot boundary.

Does sustainability certification make a lot EUDR compliant?

No. Certification schemes can help a supplier collect farm data, but the operator still has to carry out its own due diligence and file plot-level geolocation for each lot. Some of our coffee and cacao lots are sourced from Rainforest Alliance certified partner farms, and we treat that as supporting information, not as an EUDR filing.

Is Indonesia a high-risk country under the EUDR?

No. Indonesia is classified as standard risk under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1093. Importers must still carry out full due diligence, and authorities check at least 3% of operators sourcing from standard-risk countries each year.
